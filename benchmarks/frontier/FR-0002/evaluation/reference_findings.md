# Reference finding set F

Assess coverage by scientific meaning, without requiring identical wording. This set covers primary and secondary conclusions in the main text, supplementary tables and extended data. Purely methodological statements and general limitations are not treated as findings.

| ID | Authors' conclusion |
|---|---|
| F1 | The baseline gut resistome is already enriched for tetracycline ARGs: they are detected in every day-0 DNA sample and most RNA samples. They account for the largest share of DNA resistome mass but initially a smaller share of the expressed RNA resistome. |
| F2 | Doxy-PEP use over 6 months is associated with an increased proportion of total resistome mass attributable to tetracycline ARGs, in both DNA and RNA. |
| F3 | Active expression of tetracycline ARGs increases after 6 months, with a larger change and stronger statistical evidence at RNA level than DNA level. |
| F4 | DNA tetracycline ARG richness does not increase significantly over time, while the number of detectably expressed tetracycline ARGs in RNA increases. Proportional expansion is more consistent with expansion of existing ARGs/carrier bacteria than acquisition of many new ARGs. |
| F5 | Ribosomal target protection is the most common tetracycline-resistance mechanism in both DNA and RNA. |
| F6 | Other non-tetracycline ARG classes do not show widespread proportional increases, providing no evidence of broad co-selection. |
| F7 | Increases in DNA and RNA tetracycline ARG proportions persist after adjustment for HIV status and cephalosporin treatment days. |
| F8 | The adjusted model shows a decrease in the RNA MLS ARG proportion. The DNA MLS point estimate is also negative, but its interval crosses zero. |
| F9 | Absolute DNA tetracycline ARG abundance does not increase significantly, whereas RNA tetracycline ARG expression abundance increases. |
| F10 | CTX-M is detected in only a few month-6 samples and not at baseline; KPC, NDM, VIM and OXA-48 are not detected. |
| F11 | DNA tetracycline ARG richness is unrelated to doxycycline dose, while its proportion has a weak positive dose association. |
| F12 | RNA tetracycline ARG richness and proportion both correlate positively with doxycycline dose, with the stronger association for proportion. |
| F13 | Categorical dose analysis finds significant resistome changes mainly among participants with more than 25 cumulative doxycycline doses; not every user shows a measurable change. |
| F14 | With dose modeled continuously, the DNA tetracycline ARG proportion association is no longer significant, while positive associations with RNA richness and proportion persist. |
| F15 | The paired subset confirms increased tetracycline ARG proportions in both DNA and RNA in the doxy-PEP arm, without showing increased richness. |
| F16 | Paired samples show both acquisition and loss of individual tetracycline ARGs, with turnover in both study arms. |
| F17 | Doxy-PEP over 6 months has little effect on overall gut bacterial mass; neither DNA nor RNA shows consistent between-arm or within-arm temporal differences. |
| F18 | Overall bacterial alpha diversity shows no consistent temporal change. Higher RNA alpha diversity in standard care than doxy-PEP at month 6 is a cross-sectional difference; baseline and within-arm temporal comparisons do not support a sustained change. |
| F19 | Bacterial-community beta diversity at month 6 does not differ significantly between doxy-PEP and standard care; overall taxonomic composition remains largely stable. |
| F20 | Three differential-abundance methods do not consistently identify bacterial genera changing between day 0 and month 6. |
| F21 | Abundances of *Clostridioides difficile*, *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* and *Mycoplasma genitalium* do not change significantly; *Chlamydia trachomatis* shows only a nonsignificant suggestion of decline. |
| F22 | Between-arm beta diversity differs for the expressed RNA resistome at month 6 but not for the DNA resistome, consistent with transcriptional changes in the tetracycline class. |
| F23 | Several tetracycline ARGs have positive abundance correlations with commensal or potentially pathogenic bacterial genera, with no significant negative correlations. |
| F24 | Correlations between commensal genera such as Faecalibacterium and Gardnerella and tetracycline target-protection ARGs strengthen from day 0 to month 6. |
| F25 | Correlations of genera such as Bacteroides and Escherichia with some tetracycline ARGs show moderate-to-large increases; the Staphylococcus–Tet(K) correlation increases slightly. |
| F26 | Abundance correlations suggest that some tetracycline ARG expansion may involve potential pathogens. Short-read data cannot establish ARG host species, horizontal transfer or clinical antibiotic-resistant infection. |
| F27 | Conclusions are limited to a sequencing subset enriched for higher doxy-PEP use, with substantial quality-control losses and only 6 months of follow-up. They cannot be directly generalized to average users, other anatomical sites or longer exposure. |
