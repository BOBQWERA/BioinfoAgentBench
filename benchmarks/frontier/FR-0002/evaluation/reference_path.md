# Authors' analytical path P*

Article: Chu VT, Glascock A, et al. *Impact of doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis for sexually transmitted infections on the gut microbiome and antimicrobial resistome*. Nature Medicine. Online 2024-10-03. DOI: 10.1038/s41591-024-03274-2.

Nodes follow the article's narrative order. The basis column refers only to preceding nodes, the task card or author materials. Anchor IDs correspond to `anchors.json`.

| ID | Type | Content | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | Q | Estimand: effects of 6 months of doxy-PEP exposure, relative to baseline and standard care, on rectal resistome and bacterial-microbiome composition, abundance, expression and diversity in the sequenced DoxyPEP trial subset. The population comprises participants supplying qualified DNA/RNA samples. Scales include proportions, richness/diversity, between-arm or temporal differences, and dose correlations. | Task card; study objectives |
| D1 | D | Select 150 trial participants (100 doxy-PEP, 50 standard care), applying study-arm/HIV-status quotas and requiring day-0/month-6 sample availability. Use simple random sampling for standard care and prioritize higher reported use in doxy-PEP. | Q1; trial and sample availability |
| D2 | D | Perform nucleic-acid extraction, library preparation and minimum nucleic-acid/sequencing quality control on self-collected rectal swabs. Exclude unavailable/incomplete samples or those below minimum quality. | D1; laboratory quality control |
| E1 | E | DNA: 89 participants, 127 qualified samples (58 at day 0, 69 at month 6). RNA: 70 participants, 86 qualified samples (26 at day 0, 60 at month 6). [A1–A4] | D2; Fig. 1 |
| E2 | E | In month-6 DNA samples, median doxycycline doses are 0 (IQR 0–7) for standard care and 42 (27–64) for doxy-PEP. RNA values are 0 (0–7) and 42 (29–65). Cephalosporin exposure is more common in standard care. | D2; Table 1 |
| H1 | H | The sequencing subset includes different visits, repeated participant measurements, incomplete DNA/RNA overlap and imbalanced exposure to other antimicrobials between arms. Main and sensitivity analyses must address these structures separately. | E1; E2 |
| M1 | M | Align nonhost reads to references: NCBI NT/NR for microbes and CARD for resistance genes. Analyze DNA and RNA separately. | Q1; assay definitions |
| M2 | M | Fit negative-binomial background distributions using negative controls and assess background by batch and taxon or ARG. Exclude calls with background-association P≥0.05 and control FDR using Benjamini–Hochberg. | M1; negative controls and batch structure |
| D3 | D | Retain patient samples for ARG analysis. Assign each ARG to a resistance class and tetracycline scope; calculate total ARG mass, class-specific mass, DPM and class proportions of total resistome mass per sample. | M1; M2 |
| E3 | E | At baseline, 41 tetracycline ARGs are detected in DNA and 17 in RNA. At least one is present in 58/58 DNA samples and 22/26 RNA samples (85%). [A5–A8] | D3; Extended Data Fig. 1 |
| E4 | E | At baseline, tetracycline ARGs account for approximately 46% of DNA resistome mass but only 4% of expressed RNA resistome mass. Ribosomal target protection is the most common tetracycline-resistance mechanism. | D3; Extended Data Figs. 1–2 |
| M3 | M | Calculate total resistome mass, Shannon alpha diversity and Bray–Curtis beta diversity. Use two-sided rank-sum tests for between-arm and temporal comparisons, PERMANOVA for beta diversity, and assess within-group dispersion. Apply BH-FDR for multiple comparisons. | Q1; H1; D3 |
| E5 | E | Total resistome mass and alpha diversity in DNA/RNA show no significant between-arm or temporal differences. At month 6, DNA resistome beta diversity does not differ between arms (PERMANOVA P=0.82), while expressed RNA resistome composition does (adjusted P=1.8×10^-2). | M3; Fig. 2 |
| H2 | H | Total mass and overall diversity are largely stable, but RNA resistome composition differs; examine individual ARG classes, richness and proportions. | E5 |
| D4 | D | Retain classes whose median proportion exceeds 1% in any study-arm × visit × assay subgroup. Exclude multiclass tetracycline efflux pumps from main class comparisons because their overall proportion is below 1%. | H2; Supplementary Table 2 |
| M4 | M | Compare tetracycline ARG richness and its share of total resistome mass across arms/visits, and temporal changes in the five main ARG classes within doxy-PEP. Use two-sided rank-sum tests with BH-FDR. | D4; H2 |
| E6 | E | Within doxy-PEP, DNA tetracycline ARG richness does not change significantly (adjusted P=0.12), whereas the number of detectably expressed tetracycline ARGs in RNA increases (adjusted P=1.5×10^-2). | M4; Fig. 3a,b |
| E7 | E | Within doxy-PEP, tetracycline ARG proportion rises from approximately 46% to 51% in DNA (adjusted P=3.0×10^-2) and from approximately 4% to 15% in RNA (adjusted P=3.6×10^-6). Other non-tetracycline classes show no proportional increases. | M4; Fig. 3c–f |
| H3 | H | Tetracycline-class proportional and expression changes differ from other classes. Check whether they persist after accounting for HIV status and between-arm differences in cephalosporin exposure. | E2; E6; E7 |
| M5 | M | Within doxy-PEP, fit zero/one-inflated beta regression by ARG class. The outcome is the class proportion of total resistome mass; the main predictor is month 6 versus day 0, adjusted for HIV status and cephalosporin treatment days. Round input proportions to two decimal places. | H3; proportional outcomes include 0/1 boundaries |
| E8 | E | The adjusted month-6 coefficient for DNA tetracycline proportion is μ=0.45 (95% CI 0.19–0.70, P=9.2×10^-4); for RNA it is μ=1.5 (0.94–2.00, P=1.7×10^-6). [A9–A10] | M5; Supplementary Table 1 |
| E9 | E | Adjusted RNA MLS proportion decreases: μ=-0.76 (95% CI -1.37 to -0.15, P=1.9×10^-2). The DNA estimate is -0.23 (-0.48 to 0.25, P=8.2×10^-2). [A11] | M5; Supplementary Table 1 |
| M6 | M | Separately examine detection of beta-lactamase ARGs of public-health concern (CTX-M, KPC, NDM, VIM, OXA-48) at baseline and month 6. | H3; co-selection risk |
| E10 | E | CTX-M is detected at month 6 in 1 standard-care and 3 doxy-PEP participants, but not at baseline. KPC, NDM, VIM and OXA-48 are not detected in any sample. | M6; main-text Results |
| H4 | H | Temporal comparisons support tetracycline selection, but actual use varies widely; assess dose–response relationships. | E2; E7; E8 |
| D5 | D | Define cumulative exposure by counting each 200 mg PEP administration as 1 dose and each therapeutic doxycycline day as 1. Categorize exposure as 0, 1–25, 26–50 and >50. Use all qualified samples, assigning 0 doses to every day-0 sample. | H4; exposure records |
| M7 | M | Use Spearman rank correlations to assess monotonic relationships between dose category and tetracycline ARG richness/proportion. Use rank-sum tests and BH-FDR for comparisons between categories. | D5; nonnormal outcomes with many 0 values |
| E11 | E | DNA richness is not associated with dose (ρ=0.027, P=0.76); proportion has a weak positive association (ρ=0.23, P=9.0×10^-3). [A12] | M7; Fig. 4a,b |
| E12 | E | RNA richness (ρ=0.39, P=2.2×10^-4) and proportion (ρ=0.55, P=3.7×10^-8) both correlate positively with dose. Significant increases between categories occur mainly above 25 cumulative doses. [A13–A14] | M7; Fig. 4c,d |
| M8 | M | Model cumulative dose continuously: linear regression for richness and zero/one-inflated beta regression for proportion, with 95% CIs for fitted curves. | E11; E12; alternative dose specification |
| E13 | E | With continuous dose, the DNA tetracycline-proportion association is no longer significant. Positive associations persist for RNA richness (P=1.2×10^-4) and RNA tetracycline proportion (P=1.8×10^-3). | M8; Extended Data Fig. 4 |
| H5 | H | Unpaired temporal comparisons may be affected by changing sample composition; confirm results within the same participants. | E1; E7 |
| D6 | D | Construct paired day-0/month-6 subsets: 38 DNA pairs (17 standard care, 21 doxy-PEP) and 16 RNA pairs (7 standard care, 9 doxy-PEP). | H5; repeated `participant_id` structure |
| M9 | M | Within each arm, compare tetracycline ARG richness and proportion using two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with multiple-comparison adjustment. | D6 |
| E14 | E | Tetracycline ARG richness does not change significantly in the paired subset. Within doxy-PEP, proportion rises from approximately 45% to 51% in DNA (adjusted P=2.0×10^-2) and from approximately 6% to 26% in RNA (adjusted P=1.6×10^-2). Both arms show acquisition and loss of individual tetracycline ARGs. | M9; Fig. 5 |
| H6 | H | Resistome changes do not necessarily imply changes in the overall bacterial community. Independently assess bacterial mass, alpha/beta diversity and taxon abundance. | E5; E7 |
| D7 | D | Build sample × taxon abundance matrices from genus-level calls. Analyze DNA and RNA separately and normalize bacterial mass using total sample reads and ERCC standards. | H6; M1; sample read counts |
| M10 | M | Assess the bacterial microbiome using Shannon indices, Bray–Curtis distances and PERMANOVA. Use two-sided rank-sum tests with BH-FDR for between-arm/temporal comparisons of mass and alpha diversity. | D7; repeated measurements and study-arm structure |
| E15 | E | Bacterial mass, DNA alpha diversity and within-arm temporal comparisons show no significant differences. RNA alpha diversity at month 6 is higher in standard care than doxy-PEP (adjusted P=4.5×10^-2), but neither baseline between-arm comparisons nor within-arm temporal comparisons differ. Month-6 DNA/RNA beta diversity does not differ between arms. | M10; Extended Data Fig. 5 |
| M11 | M | Within doxy-PEP, assess genus-level differential abundance with DESeq2, metagenomeSeq and ALDEx2, interpreting only consistent findings as robust. Separately assess four pathogens of interest with two-sided rank-sum tests and multiple-comparison adjustment. | H6; zero-containing taxon counts and method dependence |
| E16 | E | The three methods identify no consistent differential genera. *C. difficile*, *N. gonorrhoeae* and *M. genitalium* show no significant temporal changes. *C. trachomatis* shows a nonsignificant suggestion of decline (P=0.06). | M11; Extended Data Fig. 6 |
| H7 | H | Overall taxonomic composition remains stable while tetracycline ARG proportions expand. Within-sample abundance correlations may identify genera that covary with ARGs. | E7; E15; E16 |
| D8 | D | Use day-0/month-6 DNA data from doxy-PEP and the 50 most abundant bacterial genera to construct paired matrices of tetracycline ARG DPM and genus RPM. | H7; DNA contains both ARG and taxonomic abundances |
| M12 | M | Calculate Spearman correlations separately by visit, correcting for multiple comparisons. For combinations significant at month 6, calculate ΔSCC=ρ_month6−ρ_day0. | D8 |
| E17 | E | Several positive tetracycline ARG–genus correlations occur at day 0 and month 6, with no significant negative correlations. Target-protection ARG correlations with several commensal genera strengthen. Bacteroides, Escherichia and other genera show moderate-to-large increases for particular ARGs, and Staphylococcus–Tet(K) increases slightly. | M12; Extended Data Figs. 7–8 |
| C1 | C | In this 6-month sequencing subset, doxy-PEP is associated with relative expansion of tetracycline ARGs in the resistome and increased active expression, with stronger changes at RNA level. | E7; E8; E12; E14 |
| C2 | C | Effects are mainly restricted to tetracycline resistance, with no widespread co-selection of other ARG classes. Individual MLS decreases and rare beta-lactamase detections do not support broad co-expansion. | E7; E9; E10 |
| C3 | C | DNA tetracycline ARG richness does not increase, suggesting relative expansion of existing ARGs/carrier bacteria rather than acquisition of many new ARGs. This mechanism remains an inference. | E6; E7; E14 |
| C4 | C | Tetracycline resistome changes are dose-dependent, particularly at RNA-expression level and with higher cumulative use. Not every doxy-PEP user necessarily shows a measurable change. | E11; E12; E13 |
| C5 | C | At 6 months, overall gut bacterial mass, alpha/beta diversity and taxonomic composition are only modestly affected, with no consistent genus-level changes. | E15; E16 |
| C6 | C | Abundance correlations between tetracycline ARGs and some commensal/potentially pathogenic genera strengthen over time. Short-read correlation analysis cannot assign an ARG conclusively to a species or demonstrate horizontal transfer or clinical antibiotic-resistant infection. | E17; short-read sequencing limitations |
| C7 | C | Conclusions apply only to a 6-month sequencing subset enriched for high doxy-PEP use and affected by substantial quality-control losses. Therapeutic doxycycline exposure in standard care, missing dose-to-sampling intervals and absence of other anatomical-site data further limit generalizability. | D1; D2; E2; study limitations |
